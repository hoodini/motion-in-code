01

Data and charts

The moment the film proves something: growth, results, progress.

A good launch film answers the question "Why should I believe you?", and most of the time the answer is a number. In the film for Ketzev (Hebrew for "rhythm"), a fictional fitness app, this is the moment the user sees how far they came this month. The four elements here are four ways to show a number: a line that unfolds over time, a comparison between days, one big number, and a percentage of a goal.

The library we use here is Recharts: a collection of React components for charts, which draws them as SVG (that is, vector graphics, sharp at any zoom). Under the hood it borrows parts of a library called D3 for the math: where each tick mark goes, how to smooth a curve. It's free under the MIT license, and the current version is 3. The main alternatives, and when to pick them:

  • Chart.js: draws on Canvas and doesn't need React. Fast and simple, less flexible in styling. MIT.
  • Apache ECharts: hundreds of chart types, maps included. Heavier. Apache 2.0.
  • D3: the low-level layer itself. Full control over every pixel, and a lot more code. ISC.

The rule for choosing: if the project is in React and the chart is a standard one, Recharts. If we need a shape that has no name, D3. Either way, the built-in animation is off and everything is computed from t.

01.1

An area chart that draws itself

RechartsReactSVGMIT

What you see

30 days of workout minutes in the Ketzev app. The line draws itself from left to right, an orange dot rides its tip, and at the end a dashed goal line lands: 60 minutes. The viewer watches the trend being built instead of receiving it ready-made.

Try it

Check "Let Recharts animate on its own". Now the library animates by the wall clock, and the scrubber no longer controls it. That's exactly the animation that would break during capture.

How it works

  • On every frame we compute from t how much of the line is already revealed, and pass Recharts only those points, plus one in-between point so the tip moves smoothly instead of jumping a whole day at a time.
  • isAnimationActive={false} turns off the library's own animation. The reveal is ours.
  • type="monotone" smooths the line without overshooting the data, so we don't invent peaks that never happened.
  • The fill is a gradient from orange to transparent, and the goal is a ReferenceLine whose thickness grows on a spring.
When to useThe proof moment: "look how far you've come". One clear trend, with an ending that lands on the beat.
When not toMore than three lines, or exact values the viewer needs to read in two seconds. In that case, one big number (01.3) works better.

01.2

Bars that land on the beat

RechartsBarChartMIT

What you see

A week of workouts. Each bar jumps up on its own beat, half a second after the one before it, overshoots its height a little and settles back. Today's bar is painted in the brand's "fire" gradient (yellow, orange, red). The days run left to right, Sunday to Saturday. (If your film is in Hebrew or Arabic, which read right to left, reverse the order so the week starts on the right.)

How it works

  • The height of each bar = its value × spring(t − beatTime), where beatTime is the moment of that bar's own beat. We subtract the beat time to give each bar its own clock that starts at zero: before its beat the result is negative, the spring returns 0, and the bar hasn't started yet. The spring overshoots 1 a little and comes back, which is why the bar "lands" instead of stopping dead.
  • The number above a bar appears only once the bar is 60% of the way up, so the number doesn't jump ahead of it.
  • Today's color comes from Cell, a Recharts component that lets each bar have its own color.
When to useComparing 4 to 12 items and highlighting one of them. Perfect for a musical rhythm, because every bar is a beat.
When not toMore than 12 bars: they get too thin to read on a phone screen, and the animation drags on too long.

01.3

Counters that count up

CSStabular-numsIntl.NumberFormat

What you see

Three numbers that run from zero to their final value, one after another, and land with a small hop past the value and back. No library at all: it's just a number computed from t and written into an element.

Try it

Uncheck "Fixed-width digits" and watch the numbers shake while they count.

Why they shake

In many fonts, for example Inter, the font the numbers here are set in, the digit 1 is narrower than the digit 8. These are called proportional digits. When the number changes every frame, its width changes, and everything around it moves. The fix is a font feature called tabular-nums: every digit gets the same width, like in a table. (This works only if the font includes such digits; Inter does.) One line of CSS: font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums.

And for the thousands separators (1,248) we use Intl.NumberFormat, the browser's built-in tool for formatting numbers the way each language writes them.

When to useOne number that is the story: "1,248 minutes". It reads instantly, even on a phone.
When not toMore than three counters at the same moment: the eye doesn't know where to look.

01.4

Progress ring

SVGstroke-dasharray

What you see

A ring that fills to 78% of the weekly goal, with the number counting up in the center. The ring we know from fitness apps, drawn in code, with no library at all.

Try it

Check "Show the full outline" and see that the ring is really one long dashed line.

How it works

In SVG we can turn a circle's outline into a dashed line. stroke-dasharray sets the length of the dash and also the length of the gap after it, and stroke-dashoffset shifts where the pattern starts. The trick: make the dash and the gap both as long as the whole circumference (2πr: two times pi, about 6.28, times the radius r). When we shift the dash, the gap slides into its place, and the gap is nothing, so only part of the dash stays visible:

offset = circumference × (1 − progress). When progress is 0, the whole dash sits off the circle. At 1, it covers everything. The numbers update live in the dark box. And we rotate the circle by −90°, because in SVG a circle starts at 3 o'clock, and we want it to start at 12.

When to useA percentage of a goal, a countdown, loading. A shape everyone knows from their watch and their phone.
When not toComparing several values: nested rings are hard to read. Bars (01.2) are clearer.

02

Real UI with shadcn/ui

A product film needs an app that looks real. Here's how to get one without designing it from scratch.

The most important scene in Ketzev's film is a tour of the app: someone types, clicks, switches a tab, and we see that the product works. For it to look like a real app, we build it from real interface components. Three concepts, each one stacked on the one before:

  • Tailwind CSS is a way to write styling with classes: a class is a label we stick on an element, and the CSS decides what each label does. In Tailwind every label does one small thing, so instead of writing a separate CSS file, we write rounded-full bg-primary px-4: fully rounded, in the primary color, with horizontal padding.
  • Radix is a library of components with no styling at all, which handles all the behavior: the keyboard, focus, and screen readers (software that reads the screen aloud for people with visual impairments). A tab that works properly with the Tab key, for example.
  • shadcn/ui combines the two. It describes itself as "not a component library": instead of installing a package, we copy each component's code into the project (npx shadcn add button), and it's ours to change. That's exactly what lets us adapt it for video.

shadcn/ui is very popular, which is why it looks "like a real app". For the same reason, it can also look generic. In the demo below, every component got the Phoenix colors, the YUV.AI brand palette (navy, orange, gold), and the brand fonts (Anton for headlines, Inter for text), and that's what makes them ours.

What you see

A Ketzev screen built from nine real shadcn/ui components. The list on the side marks which component is acting right now. Drag the scrubber backward and watch every component return to exactly the state it had at that moment, the tab and the switch included.

Controlled components: the key idea here

A regular switch remembers by itself whether it's on. This is called internal state. In a film that's a problem: if the switch "remembers" it was turned on and we jump back in time, it stays on. That's why every component here is controlled: it decides nothing on its own. On every frame we tell it from the outside what its state is, for example checked={t > 6.6}.

How it works

  • The components were copied from shadcn/ui, and every transition class was deleted from them. A class like that makes the browser animate changes by itself, on the wall clock.
  • Every value is a function of t: the typed text, the active tab, the switch position, the slider value.
  • The mouse cursor computes where the real button sits on the screen and glides to it, slowing down as it arrives.
  • The colors are defined once as variables (--primary, --accent). A variable is a name for a value we store once, and every element "inherits" the variables of the element that wraps it. So we define them once at the top, and all the components get them. That's how we swap a brand in one line.
When to useA product film, a feature demo, "here's how it works". When the viewer needs to believe there's a real app.
When not toWhen there are real screenshots and a product that already exists: use the real screens, and rebuild only what moves.
