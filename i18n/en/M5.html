Anatomy of the prompt: the six blocks, and why each one works on an LLM

All of zero's prompts are built on exactly the same skeleton. Click a block.

The principle that ties all six blocks togetherWhen we don't tell a language model exactly what we want, it gives us the average of what it saw in training. In motion design, the average is exactly "the template": dark background, glow, particles, fade. The prompt from zero doesn't add "creativity". It deletes the average options (the banned list), sets the numbers (colors, BPM, seconds, sizes), passes on engineering knowledge (build), saves us the bugs he has already hit (gotchas), and brings a human into the loop cheaply (start). It's a prompt written like a director's technical spec, not like a request.

The template: our own prompt in six blocks

This is the skeleton of zero's three prompts, with square brackets where his content was. We fill every [bracket] with an exact number or sentence, delete the brackets and paste it into Claude Code. Everything outside the brackets is knowledge that already worked in all three films (closed-form springs, a log-space camera zoom, 8 subframes), so it's best left as it is.

template · fill the [brackets] · paste into Claude Code
<inputs>
Ask me for: [my product and the one thing a user does in it], [6 to 12 photos, screenshots or product shots], a royalty-free song around [BPM] BPM with a clear drop (Mixkit, free for commercial use), and [my brand mark or wordmark for the end card]. If I skip any, use these defaults: [default story], [default photo source], [default song].
</inputs>

<direction>
A [length] second [product launch / explainer / teaser] film, [1920x1080 / 1080x1920 / 1440x1440] at 60fps, 2D only, one continuous take. Every scene is made out of the previous one: nothing fades, blurs or cuts. Objects change shape instead: [3 or 4 motion verbs from the dictionary, e.g. text rises out of a mask line, a button grows into a page, a color floods out of one object and shrinks back into another]. The camera makes one move per scene on eased keyframes and never zooms in and out back to back.
Look: [canvas color #hex], [UI color #hex], [one accent #hex], [optional second accent #hex]. [One signature trick, e.g. an iridescent glow around the composer, liquid glass over the photos]. [UI font] for UI, [display font] for [the headline / the wordmark].
Banned: crossfades, blur-ins, 3D, particles, glows, holds longer than 1s, camera moves that reverse direction, anything that looks like a template, [what the average film of this genre always does].
</direction>

<structure>
[BPM] BPM, [N] beats ([length]s). The song starts [K] beats before its drop.
Beats [0-a], [scene name]: [what is on screen, what the cursor does, the exact on-screen text in quotes].
Beat [a], [event]: [X] grows into [Y], [how, in numbers: size, seconds, colors].
Beats [a-b], [scene name]: [...]
Beat [drop], the drop: [the biggest change in the film, landing exactly on the drop].
Beats [b-c], [scene name]: [...]
Beats [c-end], outro: [the last object] becomes [the brand mark], and [the wordmark] [is revealed how]. [Optional: last frame = first frame.]
</structure>

<build>
1. One HTML file, [width]x[height]. Every style is computed from time inside seek(t): no CSS transitions, no timers, no state between frames.
2. The camera is one transform on a container, keyed [time, zoom, x, y] with eased segments and zoom interpolated in log space.
3. Springs are closed-form step responses. A value with many targets is the sum of one spring per change, so it stays a pure function of time.
4. Every handoff between scenes is a shared element: [which object carries what into the next scene].
5. [How to build the signature trick, in engineering terms.]
6. Sound: a downloaded SFX for every event (Mixkit), never synthesized: [typing, pops, the drop impact, a whoosh, a click, a success tone...]. Place each by its measured peak and loudnorm to -14 LUFS.
7. Render with Playwright: 8 subframes per frame blended with ffmpeg tmix, 60fps. Check one frame per beat, then scan the whole video for single-frame pops (frame-difference spikes 3x their neighbours).
</build>

<gotchas>
4 subframes leave ghost copies on fast moves, so use 8 and slow the move down. A flood must clear the farthest corner and take about 0.35s, or it reads as a flash. Set z-index on every layer. Never let the camera chase a wrapping text cursor: fixed lines only. Declare every variable before the first seek() runs. [Any bug we hit last time.]
</gotchas>

<start>
Ask me for the inputs, then show me the beat map and 4 stills ([opening], [drop], [middle], [outro]) before you write the full film.
</start>
Before we send it: six checks
  • Numbers instead of adjectives. Not "fast" but 0.35 seconds, not "a warm background" but #f5f5f2. An adjective leaves the choice to the model, and it will pick the average.
  • A banned list that fits the genre. What does the average film of this kind always do? That is what we ban, explicitly.
  • Every scene written as X grows into Y. A scene that starts from nothing means a transition is missing, and that is where the model will put a fade.
  • The drop on a specific beat. We write which beat, and what the biggest change on it is.
  • One signature trick. One thing people remember, like the glow around the composer or the liquid glass. Not three.
  • A stills gate at the end. A beat map and 4 stills before the full film, because a fix at that stage costs a minute, not a whole render.

The names for the brackets (composer, morph, flood, push in) come from the interface dictionary, where every component and motion is shown live. And if we'd rather assemble the prompt from ready-made building blocks, there is a prompt builder in the catalog.

Bonus: the 3D fly-over prompt

Exactly the same skeleton, but here zero breaks his own "2D only" rule, on purpose.

Full prompt + explanation
original · paste into Claude Code

    

  

  
What's new here compared with the previous three prompts:

  
1. A camera driven by speed, not by positions

  
"Drive the camera from one speed curve, never from a position key every half second... Integrate it into the page position." Instead of telling the camera "at 0.5 seconds be at y=300, at 1.0 be at y=700", we define how fast it travels at every moment, and add up (integrate) that speed over time to get the position. Why? Because when we connect fixed positions with a smooth curve, the speed between the keys turns "wavy": it speeds up and slows down around each key. The eye reads this as a glide that stalls and lurches ("stops or surges in the glide" is on the banned list). When we start from the speed, it stays exactly as flat as we defined it.

  
Speed curve → position, vs. a key every half second

    
    

  

  
2. Asymmetric depth of field

  
"blur radius = 75 × (focus − depth) / depth in front of the focus and 6 × (depth − focus) / depth behind it, capped at 120px". Depth of field = how much of the image is sharp. In a real lens, things very close to the lens blur much faster than things far away, and that's what gives the "macro" feel of an expensive miniature. The multiplier of 75 in front versus 6 behind (12.5 times more) creates exactly this: the page near the lens "melts", while whatever sits behind the focus stays almost sharp.

  
How much blur at each depth

    
    

  

  
3. Three engineering details that explain "why does mine look cheap?"

  
    
    
  • Mipmaps + 16x anisotropic filtering: A texture (here, the image of the page) seen at a steep angle and from far away gets squeezed into far fewer pixels than it actually contains. Without preparation, each frame "samples" different pixels, so distant text flickers (shimmer). Mipmaps = pre-shrunk versions of the image, made in advance. Anisotropic filtering = sampling that understands the image is stretched more in one direction than the other (because of the angle).
    • 
    
  • "drawn lowest first with no depth buffer": We draw from the lowest layer up to the highest, like a painter who paints the background first (the "painter's algorithm"). No depth buffer (the GPU's per-pixel record of what is closest to the camera), because the layers are partly transparent, and a depth buffer can't handle transparency.
    • 
    
  • "fade them into the haze colour first": A card that turns transparent reveals whatever is behind it, and in 3D that looks like a "ghost". So first we tint it to the background's haze color (it "dissolves" into the air), and only at the very end cut it to transparent.
    • 
  

  
Two things this prompt requires, worth knowing up front(1) It needs a real GPU (--use-angle=d3d11) and 16 subframes per frame, which means rendering on a local PC, not in the cloud. 14.5 × 60 × 16 = 13,920 captures (seconds × fps × subframes). (2) It starts with "open my link": it captures the real landing page, so any change to a section's height on that page shifts all the timing ("lock the page before timing anything").

What we can learn from this, including what's overstated
The method is excellent. Its headline, less so.

  

    
The claimWhat's trueWhat's overstated / missing

    
"$0"No need for After Effects or paid stock footage.Claude itself costs money (a subscription or tokens). One source reports ~90K input tokens + 15K output tokens per film ≈ $0.66 at list price. That's for one film, before revision rounds, rendering and the time we put in.

    
"0 effort" / "I don't know step 1 of motion design"No animator's hands needed.The prompts are packed with deep professional knowledge (180° shutter, logarithmic zoom, closed-form springs, LUFS). The knowledge didn't disappear: it moved into the prompt. Change the prompts without understanding them, and they break.

    
"One prompt"One prompt drives the process.In practice: reference → assets → beat map → stills → render → 2–3 rounds of notes. Another creator (@rexan_wong) who examined videos like these also concluded that "one prompt" is really a workflow.

    
"You can't tell it from a $15K video"The demos really are high quality.These are demos in one style he likes (2D, white, Apple-like). A studio also sells strategy, copy and a client process. The claim is his; nobody measured it.

    
"It's all free on Mixkit/Pexels"Both have large free libraries.Mixkit has a separate license for each type of item (music, SFX, "free" vs. "restricted" video). Pexels has restrictions on identifiable people and brands. Check every file before any commercial use.

  

  
The big pictureThe real shift here isn't "AI makes video". It's that motion design has turned into director-led software engineering: a spec, tests, targeted fixes, versions. Whoever can write the spec (taste + numbers + a banned list) and give precise notes wins. That's exactly the difference between zero's videos and "my one prompt video looked mid".

  
A note on Opus 5.5's effort levelCommunity documentation for video prompts recommends running at a high effort level (high/xhigh), not the default. Effort is the setting for how much the model thinks before it answers. On a different technical benchmark (Terminal-Bench-Science), scores improved up to xhigh and dipped slightly at max. This wasn't measured on video specifically, but it's a reasonable starting point: xhigh for planning and writing the film.
