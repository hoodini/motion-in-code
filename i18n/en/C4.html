Effects and transitions

The "AI is thinking" neon, glass, an aurora glow, and transitions without a fade.

06.1

Neon edge: "the AI is thinking"

CSSconic-gradientmask

What you see

A text field with a colored edge spinning around it and a soft halo that pulses on every beat, while a request types itself inside. This has become the standard visual cue for the moment an AI is processing a request.

How it works

  • conic-gradient is a color gradient that sweeps around a point, like the hands of a clock: every angle gets a different color. Change the starting angle, and the colors rotate.
  • mask cuts only a thin ring at the edge out of that gradient, so all that's left is a colored outline.
  • A second copy of the same gradient, blurred and placed behind, creates the halo. On every beat it briefly gets stronger.
  • The angle = t × 1.5 radians. A radian is a unit of angle, like a degree: a full turn is 2π, about 6.28 radians. At 1.5 radians per second we get a full turn about every 4.2 seconds, slow enough to feel like "thinking" rather than dizzying. On the page itself (the button at the top) the neon spins with a regular CSS animation, because nothing is being captured there. In the film, the angle comes from t.
When to useA prompt field, the main action button, one central card. One element per screen.
When not toOn everything: neon everywhere cancels itself out and turns into noise.

<gotchas>
- A CSS @keyframes rotation runs on the wall clock and breaks the capture. Set the angle from t.
- If you register --ang with @property and inherits: false, a value set on the element never reaches its ::before. Leave it unregistered (plain custom properties inherit) or register it with inherits: true.
- Keep the mask on both -webkit-mask (with -webkit-mask-composite: xor) and mask (with mask-composite: exclude).
</gotchas>

06.2

Glass card

CSSbackdrop-filter

What you see

A milky data card floating over moving blobs of color. The card itself is almost transparent; what gives it its look is the blur of whatever is behind it. Move the blur slider, and switch the glass off to see the difference.

How it works

backdrop-filter: blur() blurs what's behind the element, not the element itself. saturate() boosts the colors of that blurred background, and that's what gives it the "liquid" feel. Add a thin, semi-transparent white border, and the glass seems to have thickness. The blobs move by the sin and cos of t.

When to useAn info card over a colorful moving background, a notification over a photo, an interface in the "glass" style of modern operating systems.
When not toOver a flat, solid background: there's nothing to blur, and the card just looks gray.

</gotchas>

06.3

Aurora background

Canvas 2Dblurgrain

What you see

A background of huge, soft blobs of color drifting slowly, with a fine grain like film. Check "No blur" and the secret is out: these are five plain circles.

How it works

We draw five large circles in the brand colors, each moving along a path made of the sin and cos of t at its own speed, and blur them all with a very heavy blur (70 pixels). On top goes a faint layer of random noise.

Why the grain matters

A video stores each color channel (red, green, blue) in 8 bits, which means 256 levels. A subtle gradient, say from navy to a slightly lighter navy, uses maybe 20 of them. Across 1,000 pixels that's 20 steps, each 50 pixels wide, and the eye sees stripes (banding). The video file's compression makes it even worse. Subtle noise breaks up the stripes, because the eye averages it into one smooth tone. This technique is called dithering.

When to useA background for a headline or an end scene, a change of mood between chapters.
When not toBehind small text or a busy interface: it lowers the contrast.

- Generate the noise from a fixed seed so every frame uses the same pattern (a changing pattern looks like TV static).
</gotchas>

06.4

Four transitions without a fade

Canvas 2Dfloodmorphwipeiris

Why no fade

A fade (crossfade) is when one image slowly disappears and the next one appears underneath it. It's the first transition in every editing app, which is exactly why it looks like a "template". zero's rule: every scene is born from an object that's already on screen. That way the eye has something to follow instead of being cut off.

FLOOD: fill the frame

A button is pressed, and its color spreads out as a circle until it fills the whole screen. The final radius = the distance from the button to the farthest corner, so not a single sliver is left behind.

MORPH: change shape

A small pill widens, then grows taller and changes its corner rounding until it becomes a card. Width, height and corner radius each ride a spring of their own.

WIPE: sweep across

A new page enters from the right, led by a fire-gradient band. That band is what turns a plain wipe into something with energy.

IRIS: close to a dot

The screen closes into a shrinking circle until only a dot is left, and that dot becomes the dot in the YUV.AI logo. The radius shrinks on a logarithmic scale: in every equal stretch of time it shrinks by the same factor. In a 1920×1080 film it starts at 1,100 pixels, the distance from the center of the screen to a corner, so at first the circle covers everything, and it ends at 14, the size of the dot in the logo. Four equal steps: 1,100, 370, 125, 42, 14, each one 3 times smaller than the one before. Why not linear? A linear shrink removes the same number of pixels every frame: 18 pixels out of 1,100 are barely noticeable, but 18 out of 30 is more than half. So the circle hardly moves at first and then slams shut all at once, because the eye perceives changes in size as ratios, not as pixels.

When to useIn every transition between scenes. Pick by the object on screen: a button floods, a pill becomes a card, a dot becomes a logo.
When not toA transition that doesn't grow out of an object on screen looks like a cut, however polished it is.

</build>

- A flood radius smaller than the farthest corner leaves a sliver of the old scene.
- Linear iris shrinking crawls at the start and snaps shut at the end; use log space.
- Give each layer an explicit z-order so the flood covers the old scene but not the label it came from.
</gotchas>

How to choose, in four questions

  1. One film or a hundred? One: HTML and seek(t). A hundred from the same template: Remotion or HyperFrames.
  2. Is there a ready-made animation from a designer? Lottie fits into any of these approaches.
  3. Does it live inside an app and react to the user? Rive. At that point it's no longer a video question.
  4. Need a custom 3D model? Blender or Spline, export to GLTF, and bring it into three.js.

Prompt builder

Pick a format, a length, branding and elements from the catalog, and get one complete prompt for the whole film.

There's no AI here. The builder assembles the prompt from fixed parts, so it's easy to see exactly which sentence each choice adds: every element adds a line to each of the six blocks, for example how to build it in <build> and which bug to warn about in <gotchas>. The music tempo and the length are converted into a number of beats, and the elements are spread across the film between a four-beat opening and a four-beat ending, in story order: first the product, then the proof (charts and numbers), and finally the trust (logos). Transitions and the background don't get a beat range of their own, because they aren't scenes: they're woven through the whole film. Write field 1 in English, since it goes into the prompt word for word.
